# Ballot Swipe fact-check list: November 3, 2026 General Election

Every factual claim shown in the app, numbered so reviewers can reference them ("claim 47 is wrong because...").

## How to use this with Perplexity

Paste ONE proposition at a time (not the whole list) with this prompt:

> I am fact-checking a nonpartisan voter guide for California's November 3, 2026 election. For each numbered claim below, tell me: (1) ACCURATE, INACCURATE, or CAN'T VERIFY; (2) the correct information if it is wrong; (3) the source, preferring the Official Voter Information Guide (voterguide.sos.ca.gov), the Legislative Analyst's Office (lao.ca.gov), and Cal-Access/Secretary of State campaign finance data. Also flag any wording that sounds like it favors the YES or NO side.

Then open the source Perplexity cites and confirm it yourself. AI fact-checkers also make things up, so a claim is verified only when a person has seen it in the primary source.

## Prop 1: Housing bond

Sources to check against: [LAO analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=1&year=2026) · [Official Voter Guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/1/) · [California Budget & Policy Center](https://calbudgetcenter.org/resources/proposition-1-californias-veterans-and-affordable-housing-bond/)

1. **Official title:** Authorizes Bonds for Housing Affordability Programs
2. **Placed on ballot by:** Legislature
3. **In normal human English:** The state wants to borrow money to help build lower-cost places to live and to help veterans buy homes. Like a mortgage, the state pays it back with interest over about 25 years, using money from the state budget.
4. **A YES vote means:** The state can borrow $11.25 billion for affordable housing and veterans' home loans.
5. **A NO vote means:** The state does not borrow this money for these housing programs.
6. **Supporters say:** California is short on homes people can afford. This builds them and helps veterans buy homes.
7. **Opponents say:** This adds more state debt and doesn't fix why homes are so expensive to build.
8. **What it does (1):** $10 billion goes to state housing programs: affordable rental apartments, help buying a first home, and housing for farmworkers, seniors, college students, and people experiencing homelessness.
9. **What it does (2):** About $5.1 billion of that goes to the main state program that builds affordable apartment buildings, and $1.15 billion to supportive housing (housing with on-site services).
10. **What it does (3):** $1.25 billion goes to the CalVet Home Loan program. Veterans repay those loans through their mortgage payments.
11. **Cost to the state:** The state would pay about $500 million to $600 million a year for roughly 25 years to repay the bonds. The LAO estimates the money could help fund up to 40,000 rental homes.
12. **Named supporters:** California YIMBY
13. **Named opponents:** Some Republican state legislators

## Prop 2: Rainy day fund

Sources to check against: [LAO analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=2&year=2026) · [Official Voter Guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/2/) · [CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/california-voter-guide-2026/proposition-2-rainy-day-fund/)

16. **Official title:** Increases State's Rainy Day Fund
17. **Placed on ballot by:** Legislature
18. **In normal human English:** California has a savings account for bad years. This lets the state save twice as much as it can now. The part people argue about: money put into savings wouldn't count toward a limit that sometimes sends extra money back to taxpayers.
19. **A YES vote means:** The state can save up to 20% of its general tax revenue in reserves, up from 10%.
20. **A NO vote means:** The current savings cap of 10% stays.
21. **Supporters say:** Saving more in good years protects schools, health care, and public safety when a recession hits.
22. **Opponents say:** It creates a loophole around the state spending limit and makes taxpayer rebates less likely.
23. **What it does (1):** Raises the cap on the state's main savings account from 10% to 20% of General Fund tax revenue.
24. **What it does (2):** Money put into savings would no longer count toward the state spending limit (the "Gann limit"), which normally requires extra money to be returned to taxpayers or given to schools.
25. **What it does (3):** Allows some tax revenue to be used to pay down the state's roughly $20 billion federal unemployment insurance debt.
26. **Cost to the state:** No new tax and no tax cut. It changes how much the state can set aside in savings instead of spending or returning.
27. **Named supporters:** CA Professional Firefighters; LA Area Chamber of Commerce; Gov. Gavin Newsom
28. **Named opponents:** Authors of the official ballot argument against (names not yet verified)

## Prop 3: High-income tax extension

Sources to check against: [LAO analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=3&year=2026) · [Official Voter Guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/3/) · [KPBS explainer](https://www.kpbs.org/news/politics/2026/09/23/proposition-3-make-income-taxes-on-higher-earners-permanent-to-fund-education-and-healthcare)

31. **Official title:** Provides Permanent Funding for Schools and Health Care by Extending Existing Tax on High Incomes
32. **Placed on ballot by:** Legislature
33. **In normal human English:** Since 2012, people who make a lot (roughly $370,000 or more a year) have paid a little extra state income tax, and a big share goes to schools. That extra tax is set to end in 2030. This makes it permanent. Nobody's tax goes up from what they pay today.
34. **A YES vote means:** Extra income tax rates on high earners, set to expire in 2030, become permanent.
35. **A NO vote means:** Those extra rates end after 2030 as currently scheduled.
36. **Supporters say:** This money is a major source of school funding. Losing it means layoffs and program cuts.
37. **Opponents say:** California already has some of the highest income taxes in the country. People and jobs may leave.
38. **What it does (1):** Voters raised income tax rates on high earners in 2012 (Prop 30) and extended them in 2016 (Prop 55) until 2030.
39. **What it does (2):** This removes the 2030 end date. The rates apply to income above roughly $370,000 a year for a single filer (adjusted for inflation each year).
40. **What it does (3):** It does not raise anyone's current tax rate.
41. **Cost to the state:** Keeps about $5 billion to $15 billion a year in state revenue. The LAO estimates about 40% would typically go to schools and community colleges.
42. **Named supporters:** California Teachers Association; California State PTA; CA School Nurses Organization; Planned Parenthood Affiliates of CA
43. **Named opponents:** California Taxpayers Association; Family Business Association of California; California Hispanic Chambers of Commerce

## Prop 4: Public campaign financing

Sources to check against: [LAO analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=4&year=2026) · [Official Voter Guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/4/) · [CaVotes (League of Women Voters)](https://cavotes.org/ballot-measure/2026-prop-4/)

46. **Official title:** Repeals Prohibition Against Public Funding of Election Campaigns
47. **Placed on ballot by:** Legislature
48. **In normal human English:** Right now California bans using public money to help pay for political campaigns. This removes the ban. It doesn't start a program by itself. A city, county, or the state would have to decide later to create one.
49. **A YES vote means:** State and local governments would be allowed to create public funding programs for campaigns.
50. **A NO vote means:** The current ban on public funding of campaigns stays.
51. **Supporters say:** Public financing lowers the power of big private donors and lets candidates without wealthy backers run.
52. **Opponents say:** Taxpayers would end up paying for candidates they don't support, and the programs cost money to run.
53. **What it does (1):** Lifts the ban on using public money to help fund candidates' campaigns.
54. **What it does (2):** It does not create a program or spend money by itself. A city, county, or the state would have to create one later.
55. **What it does (3):** Any program could not use money set aside for education, transportation, or public safety.
56. **Cost to the state:** A few hundred thousand dollars a year for the state ethics commission (FPPC) to advise governments. Any program's cost would depend on later decisions.
57. **Named supporters:** League of Women Voters of CA; California Nurses Association; Consumer Watchdog
58. **Named opponents:** California Taxpayers Association; Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association; Family Business Association of California

## Prop 5: Recall elections

Sources to check against: [LAO analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=5&year=2026) · [Official Voter Guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/5/) · [CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/california-voter-guide-2026/proposition-5-recall-reform/)

61. **Official title:** Changes Recall Election Process for Statewide Officers
62. **Placed on ballot by:** Legislature
63. **In normal human English:** Today, when voters try to remove a statewide official like the governor, the same ballot lists possible replacements, and one can win with a small share of the vote. This removes that list. If the official is removed, the normal backup takes over, like the lieutenant governor stepping in for the governor.
64. **A YES vote means:** Recall ballots would no longer include a list of replacement candidates. A vacancy would be filled by the normal succession rules.
65. **A NO vote means:** Voters keep choosing a replacement on the same ballot as the recall.
66. **Supporters say:** Voters keep the power to recall, and a replacement can't win with a small share of the vote.
67. **Opponents say:** It takes away voters' right to pick the replacement and hands that choice to politicians.
68. **What it does (1):** Today, a recall ballot asks two things: remove the official? and who replaces them?
69. **What it does (2):** Under Prop 5, only the first question stays. If a governor is recalled, the lieutenant governor takes over, and voters may elect a new governor at the next statewide election.
70. **What it does (3):** Other statewide offices would be filled by appointment under existing rules. Legislative seats would get a separate special election.
71. **What it does (4):** A recalled official could not be appointed to the vacancy but could run in a later special election.
72. **Cost to the state:** Could change election costs depending on how many recalls and special elections happen. See the LAO analysis for details.
73. **Named supporters:** Authors of the official ballot argument for (names not yet verified)
74. **Named opponents:** Authors of the official ballot argument against (names not yet verified)
75. **Money, YES side:** (not yet filled in: find in SoS contribution tracker)
76. **Money, NO side:** (not yet filled in: find in SoS contribution tracker)

## Prop 37: Middle-income home loans

Sources to check against: [LAO analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=37&year=2026) · [Official Voter Guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/37/) · [California Budget & Policy Center](https://calbudgetcenter.org/resources/will-proposition-37-make-homeownership-more-affordable-its-not-guaranteed/)

77. **Official title:** Creates Loan Program for Middle-Income Buyers of Qualified New Homes
78. **Placed on ballot by:** Voters (signature petition)
79. **In normal human English:** This creates a state program that lends middle-income buyers up to 17% of the price of a newly built home, so their regular mortgage can be smaller. Buyers pay it back. The program borrows up to $25 billion, and the loan payments are designed to repay it.
80. **A YES vote means:** A state agency could issue up to $25 billion in bonds to offer second mortgages to middle-income buyers of new homes.
81. **A NO vote means:** No new state second-mortgage program is created.
82. **Supporters say:** It helps working families afford a newly built home without costing taxpayers, and gets more homes built.
83. **Opponents say:** Low down payments raise default risk, it doesn't prioritize first-time buyers, and it could push building farther out.
84. **What it does (1):** The California Housing Finance Agency would offer fixed-rate second mortgages covering up to 17% of the price of a newly built home.
85. **What it does (2):** To qualify: live in California at least a year, live in the home, earn under 200% of the local median income, and put at least 3% down.
86. **What it does (3):** Only new homes (and some converted non-residential buildings) qualify.
87. **Cost to the state:** The LAO says there would likely be no direct cost to the state, because borrowers' payments are designed to repay the bonds. If many borrowers default, there may not be enough to repay them.
88. **Named supporters:** Former Senate leader Robert Hertzberg (sponsor); California Teachers Association; United Nurses Associations of California; Construction unions
89. **Named opponents:** League of Women Voters of California; No official argument against was submitted
90. **Money, YES side:** More than $22 million raised, including from the California Association of Realtors and construction unions.
91. **Money, NO side:** No major opposition campaign reported.

## Prop 38: Immunology research bond

Sources to check against: [LAO analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=38&year=2026) · [Official Voter Guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/38/) · [CalMatters: who's behind it](https://calmatters.org/politics/elections/2026/07/proposition-38-california/)

92. **Official title:** Authorizes Bonds for Immunology Medical Research
93. **Placed on ballot by:** Voters (signature petition)
94. **In normal human English:** The state would borrow $8.4 billion for medical research on the immune system, aimed at diseases like cancer and Alzheimer's. Half the money would go to one research institute. CalMatters reports the measure's main funder co-founded the institute most likely to get that half.
95. **A YES vote means:** The state borrows $8.4 billion for research on immune-system treatments for diseases like cancer and Alzheimer's.
96. **A NO vote means:** The state does not borrow this money.
97. **Supporters say:** Immunotherapy could lead to cures, and federal research funding is being cut.
98. **Opponents say:** Half the money goes to one institute picked by the measure's authors instead of through open competition.
99. **What it does (1):** Creates an $8.4 billion bond for immunology research into cancer, heart disease, Alzheimer's, and other diseases.
100. **What it does (2):** Half goes to one research institute affiliated with the University of California. The other half is split among selected public universities and nonprofits.
101. **What it does (3):** Drugs developed with the money must be sold to Californians at a 20% discount, and 10% of drug revenue returns to the state.
102. **Cost to the state:** About $500 million to $600 million a year for roughly 20 years to repay the bonds. Some of that could be offset if the research earns money.
103. **Named supporters:** Alzheimer's Association; Michael J. Fox Foundation; ALS Association; National Multiple Sclerosis Society
104. **Named opponents:** Robert Kaplan (Stanford, former NIH official); Courage California
105. **Money, YES side:** Main backer Gary Michelson and his foundation gave at least $8.2 million; co-founder Meyer Luskin gave at least $5 million. CalMatters found the institute they co-founded is likely the only one that qualifies for the $4.2 billion share.
106. **Money, NO side:** No major opposition spending reported.

## Prop 39: Voter ID

Sources to check against: [LAO analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=39&year=2026) · [Official Voter Guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/39/) · [CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/california-voter-guide-2026/proposition-39-voter-id/)

107. **Official title:** Prohibits Citizens from Voting Unless They Present Government-Issued Identification
108. **Placed on ballot by:** Voters (signature petition)
109. **In normal human English:** You'd have to show a government ID to vote in person, and write part of an ID number on your mail ballot envelope. The state would offer free ID cards. Right now, most California voters don't have to show ID.
110. **A YES vote means:** You'd need a government ID to vote in person, and part of a government ID number to vote by mail.
111. **A NO vote means:** Voting rules stay as they are. California does not require ID for most voters.
112. **Supporters say:** Most states require ID. It builds trust in elections, and the state would give out free ID cards.
113. **Opponents say:** Eligible voters without ID, especially people with disabilities, low incomes, or who recently moved, would have a harder time voting.
114. **What it does (1):** Amends the state constitution to require a government-issued photo ID to vote in person.
115. **What it does (2):** Mail voters would write the last four digits of a government ID number on their ballot envelope.
116. **What it does (3):** The state would have to provide free voter ID cards and step up upkeep of voter registration lists.
117. **Cost to the state:** State and county costs of tens of millions to low hundreds of millions of dollars a year.
118. **Named supporters:** Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association; Latino American Political Association; California Women's Leadership Association
119. **Named opponents:** ACLU California Action; Common Cause; League of Women Voters of California
120. **Money, YES side:** Top donor is Wisconsin billionaire Richard Uihlein, about $17 million. Others include Steve Bray ($1M loan), Nicole Shanahan ($370K), and the Winklevoss twins ($250K).
121. **Money, NO side:** Donors include ACLU of Northern California, Reed Hastings ($1M), Patty Quillin ($1.5M), and Quinn Delaney ($1.5M).

## Prop 40: Billionaire tax

Sources to check against: [LAO analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=40&year=2026) · [Official Voter Guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/) · [CalMatters: how 41 & 42 could cancel it](https://calmatters.org/health/2026/09/california-wealth-tax-prop-40-41-42/)

122. **Official title:** Imposes One-Time Tax on Certain Taxpayers
123. **Placed on ballot by:** Voters (signature petition)
124. **In normal human English:** People worth more than $1 billion who lived in California on January 1, 2026 would pay a one-time tax of 5% of their wealth. Most of the money would go to health care. It's a single payment, not a yearly tax.
125. **A YES vote means:** Californians worth more than $1 billion pay a one-time 5% tax on their wealth, mostly to fund health care.
126. **A NO vote means:** No wealth tax.
127. **Supporters say:** Billionaires gained the most from recent federal tax cuts, and this money protects health care for millions.
128. **Opponents say:** Billionaires will leave the state, and California will lose tax revenue over time.
129. **What it does (1):** Applies to people who lived in California on January 1, 2026 and are worth more than $1 billion.
130. **What it does (2):** They pay a one-time tax of 5% of their net worth, due in 2027. Real estate, pensions, and retirement accounts are generally not counted.
131. **What it does (3):** Most of the money goes to state health care programs, with smaller amounts for food assistance and education.
132. **Cost to the state:** Large one-time revenue, but the amount depends heavily on how many billionaires stay. See the LAO analysis for its estimate.
133. **Named supporters:** SEIU United Healthcare Workers West (sponsor); California Democratic Party
134. **Named opponents:** Silicon Valley entrepreneurs and business groups; Some health and labor organizations
135. **Money, YES side:** About $31 million raised, mostly from SEIU-UHW.
136. **Money, NO side:** More than $56 million against, through Building a Better California, funded by Google co-founder Sergey Brin and other billionaires.
137. **Conflict note:** Props 41 and 42 are funded by the same group opposing Prop 40. If Prop 42 passes with more votes than Prop 40, Prop 40 would be cancelled even if it also passes. Prop 41 could also block it.

## Prop 41: Tax audits and spending limit

Sources to check against: [LAO analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=41&year=2026) · [Official Voter Guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/41/) · [CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/california-voter-guide-2026/proposition-41-tax-audits/)

138. **Official title:** Prohibits New State Taxes That Exclude Revenues from State Spending Limit. Requires Audits for New State Special Taxes
139. **Placed on ballot by:** Voters (signature petition)
140. **In normal human English:** This does two things. It requires regular audits of programs paid for by new special taxes. It also cancels new state taxes that skip the state spending limit, which reporters say could knock out the billionaire tax in Prop 40.
141. **A YES vote means:** New state taxes can't be exempt from the spending limit, and programs funded by new special taxes get regular audits.
142. **A NO vote means:** The rules for new state taxes stay the same.
143. **Supporters say:** Voters deserve independent audits showing whether special tax money is spent well.
144. **Opponents say:** It's written to cancel the billionaire tax and makes it harder to fund public services.
145. **What it does (1):** Cancels state taxes passed after January 1, 2026 whose revenue is exempt from the state spending limit.
146. **What it does (2):** Before a citizen initiative with a special tax goes on the ballot, the State Auditor reviews the programs it would fund.
147. **What it does (3):** Programs paid for by special taxes created after January 1, 2026 get audited every four years.
148. **Cost to the state:** Net effect unknown; it depends on future tax decisions. Audits would cost low millions of dollars a year, mostly paid from the taxes being audited.
149. **Named supporters:** California Taxpayers Association; CA Society of CPAs; CalAsian Chamber of Commerce
150. **Named opponents:** SEIU United Healthcare Workers West; Sen. Bernie Sanders
151. **Money, YES side:** Building a Better California (the group opposing Prop 40, funded by Sergey Brin and other billionaires) has raised about $131 million for Props 41 and 42 combined.
152. **Money, NO side:** (not yet filled in: find in SoS contribution tracker)
153. **Conflict note:** Reporting by CalMatters says Prop 41 could block the billionaire tax (Prop 40) even if Prop 40 passes.

## Prop 42: Ban on wealth and retroactive taxes

Sources to check against: [LAO analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=42&year=2026) · [Official Voter Guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/42/) · [CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/california-voter-guide-2026/proposition-42-property-taxes/)

154. **Official title:** Prohibits New State Personal Property Taxes and Certain Retroactive State Taxes
155. **Placed on ballot by:** Voters (signature petition)
156. **In normal human English:** This bans new state taxes on owning things like stocks, businesses, or savings, and bans taxes that reach back in time. It's aimed at stopping wealth taxes. If it gets more votes than Prop 40, Prop 40 would not take effect.
157. **A YES vote means:** The state can't create new taxes on owning things other than real estate (like stocks or businesses), or taxes that reach back in time.
158. **A NO vote means:** No new limits on these kinds of taxes.
159. **Supporters say:** People shouldn't be taxed just for owning retirement accounts, investments, or a business, or for things they did in the past.
160. **Opponents say:** It's designed by billionaires to kill the billionaire tax and block future taxes on extreme wealth.
161. **What it does (1):** Bans new state taxes on owning personal property: everything besides real estate, including business shares, investments, retirement accounts, and intellectual property.
162. **What it does (2):** Bans new state taxes that apply to things people did or owned before the tax took effect.
163. **What it does (3):** Applies to taxes enacted on or after January 1, 2026, and cancels conflicting ones.
164. **Cost to the state:** No direct cost now. It limits the state's options for raising revenue in the future.
165. **Named supporters:** CA Professional Firefighters; State Building & Construction Trades Council; AMVETS Department of CA
166. **Named opponents:** SEIU United Healthcare Workers West; Sen. Bernie Sanders
167. **Money, YES side:** Building a Better California (the group opposing Prop 40, funded by Sergey Brin and other billionaires) has raised about $131 million for Props 41 and 42 combined.
168. **Money, NO side:** (not yet filled in: find in SoS contribution tracker)
169. **Conflict note:** If Prop 42 gets more yes votes than Prop 40, Prop 40 (the billionaire tax) would be cancelled even if it also passes.

## Prop 43: Two-thirds vote for local taxes

Sources to check against: [LAO analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=43&year=2026) · [Official Voter Guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/43/) · [CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/california-voter-guide-2026/proposition-43-tax-threshold/)

170. **Official title:** Limits Voters' Ability to Raise Revenues for Local Government Services
171. **Placed on ballot by:** Voters (signature petition)
172. **In normal human English:** When residents gather signatures to put a local tax for something specific (like parks or fire services) on the ballot, it can pass today with just over half the vote. This would require a two-thirds vote instead, which makes those taxes harder to pass.
173. **A YES vote means:** Local special taxes proposed by citizen petition would need a two-thirds vote to pass instead of a simple majority.
174. **A NO vote means:** Citizen-proposed local special taxes can still pass with a simple majority.
175. **Supporters say:** It restores Prop 13's two-thirds rule and protects homeowners and renters from new taxes passed in low-turnout elections.
176. **Opponents say:** It makes it harder for communities to fund fire protection, 911, schools, roads, and housing, and lets a minority block what most voters want.
177. **What it does (1):** Special taxes are local taxes for a specific purpose, like parks or fire services.
178. **What it does (2):** A 2017 California Supreme Court ruling let special taxes proposed by citizen petition pass with 50% plus one. Taxes proposed by local governments already need two-thirds.
179. **What it does (3):** Starting January 1, 2027, citizen-proposed special taxes would also need two-thirds.
180. **Cost to the state:** Likely less local tax revenue in the future, depending on which measures would have passed with a majority but not two-thirds.
181. **Named supporters:** Anti-tax and taxpayer groups (names not yet verified)
182. **Named opponents:** Local service and housing advocates (names not yet verified)
183. **Money, YES side:** (not yet filled in: find in SoS contribution tracker)
184. **Money, NO side:** (not yet filled in: find in SoS contribution tracker)

## Prop 44: Clinic spending rule

Sources to check against: [LAO analysis](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=44&year=2026) · [Official Voter Guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/44/) · [CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/california-voter-guide-2026/proposition-44-clinic-funding/)

185. **Official title:** Community clinic spending requirement (official title not yet confirmed)
186. **Placed on ballot by:** Voters (signature petition)
187. **In normal human English:** Community clinics that serve low-income patients would have to spend at least 90 cents of every dollar they bring in on patient care, or pay fines. It's sponsored by the same health care workers' union that is behind Prop 40.
188. **A YES vote means:** Nonprofit community clinics must spend at least 90% of their revenue on patient care, or face fines.
189. **A NO vote means:** No new spending requirement for community clinics.
190. **Supporters say:** Clinics should spend money on patients, not executive pay and overhead.
191. **Opponents say:** Most clinics couldn't meet the rule. Fines could force them to cut services or close.
192. **What it does (1):** Applies to private nonprofit safety-net clinics that serve low-income patients.
193. **What it does (2):** At least 90% of yearly revenue must go to health care services. Administration and other costs are capped at 10%.
194. **What it does (3):** The attorney general decides what counts as patient care and can fine clinics that fall short.
195. **Cost to the state:** State costs to enforce the rule. Opponents estimate clinics could owe over $1.7 billion in fines in the first year; see the LAO for the neutral estimate.
196. **Named supporters:** SEIU United Healthcare Workers West (sponsor)
197. **Named opponents:** California Primary Care Association; California Medical Association; Planned Parenthood Affiliates of CA; California Hospital Association; California Democratic Party

## Prop 45: Faster environmental review

200. **Official title:** Modifies Environmental Review for Certain Projects
201. **Placed on ballot by:** Voters (signature petition)
202. **In normal human English:** California requires environmental studies before many projects get built, and lawsuits over those studies can slow things down for years. This sets deadlines for the studies and limits those lawsuits for projects like housing, transit, water, and clean energy.
203. **A YES vote means:** Many housing, transportation, water, energy, and health projects get faster environmental review and faster court decisions.
204. **A NO vote means:** Environmental review (CEQA) stays as it is.
205. **Supporters say:** Reviews and lawsuits delay badly needed housing and infrastructure for years. Deadlines get projects built.
206. **Opponents say:** It weakens protections for communities and the environment and makes the law harder to enforce.
207. **What it does (1):** Changes the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA) for eligible projects: housing, transportation, water, clean energy, health facilities, wildfire prevention, schools, and broadband.
208. **What it does (2):** Agencies get deadlines, for example about 365 business days to finish a full environmental impact report.
209. **What it does (3):** Courts would face limits on what evidence they can consider and what remedies they can order in CEQA lawsuits.
210. **Cost to the state:** See the LAO analysis. Effects depend on how many projects qualify and how agencies and courts respond.
211. **Named supporters:** California Chamber of Commerce (author); California Children's Hospital Association; California Water Association; California Council for Affordable Housing
212. **Named opponents:** Coalition for Clean Air; CA Environmental Voters; National Wildlife Federation; California Democratic Party; Labor unions
213. **Money, YES side:** Funded by gas and electric utilities, corporate PACs, and a PAC largely funded by tech executives.
